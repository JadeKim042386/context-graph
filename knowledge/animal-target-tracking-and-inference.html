Long-Range Target Tracking and Conclusion Search Strategy

This document studies how domesticated silkmoths (Bombyx mori), polar bears, and wandering albatrosses find distant mates, prey, or productive feeding areas, then transfers those principles to conclusion search in an evidence-first knowledge base. It does not copy animal behavior literally; it abstracts the problem as updating target location and answer confidence from incomplete and intermittent clues.

1. Common conclusion

None of the three species simply follows the strongest single clue in a straight line. They first locate an area where the target is likely to be found, change direction and search width when a clue is detected, and return to exploration when clues disappear or conflict. Long-range tracking is therefore a cycle of prior → detection → localization → verification → fallback.

Knowledge-base transfer: Before generating an answer, form a prior over the question's target, scope, and reference date; collect observable Claims; and narrow candidates using evidence direction and independence. When evidence breaks, do not force a conclusion. Choose the next search that is expected to add the most information.

2. Species-specific principles

2.1 Domesticated silkmoth (Bombyx mori): intermittent chemical clues and wind-referenced movement

Male Bombyx mori detects the female's species-specific sex pheromone bombykol with high sensitivity and selectivity. After detecting a short odor filament, it first moves straight upwind in a surge; when the odor disappears, it searches for the filament again through side-to-side zigzag/casting. As it gets closer, it adjusts zigzag width and speed, and uses differences in the timing and intensity of input from both antennae to choose direction. The key is not simply following a concentration gradient, but combining wind direction with the onset and disappearance of odor.

  • Evidence: sequential surge→zigzag behavior after odor stimulation and state-dependent use of optic flow have been reported.
  • Evidence: timing and intensity differences between the two antennae affect the initial travel direction.
  • Transfer: exploit while a clue is present; cross-search when it disappears. Record a failed search as a possible error in the current hypothesis's direction or width, not as an absence of information.

2.2 Polar bear: a large predator combining windscape, season, and prey distribution

Because sea-ice contours and snow restrict visibility, polar bears cannot reliably find distant prey by sight alone. They locate ringed-seal breathing holes, lairs, and haul-outs using smell and environmental clues. A Hudson Bay telemetry study found predominantly crosswind movement under low-wind, fast-travel winter conditions, consistent with broad search that increases the chance of crossing an odor plume. Strong winds can increase turbulence and make odor localization harder, while downwind movement can dominate during slow travel or high winds; the policy changes with season, time, and wind strength.

  • Some literature mentions odor detection from seal breathing holes at up to about 3 km, but this is not a direct measurement from the telemetry study and is a condition-dependent estimate.
  • Study limitation: GPS intervals of four hours or 30 minutes make it difficult to capture the brief upwind localization behavior immediately after odor detection.
  • Transfer: use windscape as a spatial prior for searchability. If the question's date, environment, or scope is unknown, lower the candidate priority of the same Claim and branch by conditions.

2.3 Wandering albatross: locating productive areas by smell and approaching with wind

Wandering albatrosses search thousands of square kilometers of ocean for dispersed squid, carrion, and surface prey. Research combined responses to fish and marine-organism odors with GPS and stomach-temperature records to analyze approach paths after initial odor detection. Because odor plumes are irregular and fragmented by turbulence, crosswind search is expected to increase encounters, followed by upwind zigzags to narrow the location after detection. In free-flight approaches, 46.8% were associated with initial olfactory detection and represented 45.5% of captured prey mass during flight.

Dimethyl sulphide (DMS) also originates from phytoplankton and can create an odor landscape associated with seafloor topography, shelf boundaries, and ocean fronts. Because DMS physiology and orientation experiments were performed on Antarctic prions rather than wandering albatrosses, they are treated only as interspecies-transfer evidence, not directly generalized to albatrosses. The odor-based approach figures for wandering albatrosses come from separate GPS and prey-capture data. The bird may therefore select a productive area where prey is likely before tracking the odor of any individual prey item.

4. Proposed conclusion-search policy

  1. Normalize the question: Split it into target (entity), relation (predicate), reference date (as-of), scope, and desired output format. Reuse an earlier result first when its canonical key matches and its dependency digest is current.
  2. Select a productive area: Search Concepts, aliases, relations, and source groups to estimate where an answer is likely. The topic prior controls search order but is not counted as conclusion evidence.
  3. Detect clues: Find direct Claim matches, semantic similarity, and date/scope matches. Every candidate must carry a Claim ID, Evidence ID, Source revision, and locator.
  4. Expand directionally: If candidates are insufficient, expand from one hop. Follow a valid relation; when evidence disappears, switch to lateral candidate search. The default maximum is two hops; further expansion is allowed only when expected information gain exceeds cost.
  5. Localize and verify: Rank directness, authority, freshness, scope, agreement, and completeness normalized to [0,1]. Check whether independent sources support the same Claim and whether an opposing Claim applies to the same validity scope.
  6. Conclude or abstain: Confirm a conclusion when each core Claim has direct Evidence and a locator and conflicts are resolved. For ties, conflicts, or missing data, present alternatives and criteria or return abstain.
  7. Feedback loop: Codex returns correct, revise, insufficient, or conflict instead of confirming a conclusion arbitrarily. On revise, add a new Claim/Evidence and invalidate the previous conclusion's dependency digest.

5. Improved score and information-gain model

Add biological inspiration to the existing simple candidate score. The final score does not automatically guarantee answer truth; it is the priority of the next candidate to read.

priority(candidate) = relevance × source_quality × freshness × scope_fit
                   × independent_agreement × locator_quality
                   + λ × expected_information_gain
                   − μ × retrieval_cost

expected_information_gain = expected_entropy_before − expected_entropy_after

expected_information_gain is the likelihood of resolving a conflict or substantially shrinking the candidate space. If two policies from different dates conflict, do not immediately choose the newest document; first search for Evidence that checks approval status, scope, and the supersedes relation. This asks how much the next sample reduces uncertainty about target location instead of continuing to follow the strongest odor.
